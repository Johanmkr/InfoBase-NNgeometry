# An Introduction to Topological Data Analysis: Fundamental and Practical Aspects for Data Scientists

**Chazal, Michel (2021)**, *arXiv preprint*. [arXiv:1710.04019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.04019)

!!! abstract "TL;DR"
    A graduate-level introduction to topological data analysis organised around one pipeline: metric data → a simplicial complex or filtration → topological invariants → features for learning. It develops simplicial complexes and the Nerve Theorem, the Mapper algorithm, geometric reconstruction and homology inference (reach, weak feature size, distance-to-measure), and persistent homology (modules, diagrams, landscapes, linear representations, stability), then the statistics of persistence (minimax rates, confidence regions, bootstrap, central tendency) and closes with two Gudhi/Python case studies. The recurring claim is that topological features are stable under Hausdorff and Gromov–Hausdorff perturbations, so estimating them reduces to estimating the support of the data distribution.

## Problem and motivation

A finite point cloud with a distance on it reveals no topology by itself, yet much of what one wants from complex data is qualitative shape: connected components, loops, voids, clusters. The survey's thesis is that topology and geometry supply "robust qualitative, and sometimes quantitative, information about the structure of data" (§1, p. 1). Its backbone is an explicit four-step pipeline (§1, pp. 1–2): (i) take data as a metric space, a distance given a priori or by an application-dependent pairwise matrix, whose choice the authors warn "may be critical"; (ii) build a continuous shape on top of it — typically a simplicial complex, or a nested family of them called a filtration, generalising the neighbouring graph; (iii) extract topological or geometric information, either by full reconstruction (a triangulation) or through cruder summaries such as persistent homology, and show it is stable under noise; (iv) turn it into features or descriptors for visualisation and learning.

The second motivation is statistical. Older topological inference was deterministic and could not separate signal from "topological noise"; treating the data as generated by an unknown distribution turns inferred features into estimators of the topological quantities of the distribution's support. Four problems are singled out (§1, p. 2): consistency and convergence rates, confidence regions and significance, scale selection, and robustness to outliers. The goal is two-fold: expose the foundations of a few selected topics — simplicial complexes (§2), Mapper (§3), geometric inference (§4), persistent homology (§5) — and demonstrate them in software, using the Python version of Gudhi (§7).

## Method

This is an expository survey, so "method" is its organisation and the technical line it follows. After fixing metric-space vocabulary, Hausdorff and Gromov–Hausdorff distances, and the theory of geometric and abstract simplicial complexes (§2), it introduces the two standard complexes: the Vietoris–Rips complex $Rips_\alpha(X)$, containing a simplex whenever all pairwise distances are at most $\alpha$, and the Čech complex $Cech_\alpha(X)$, containing it whenever the closed balls of radius $\alpha$ about its vertices have a common point; the two interleave as $Rips_\alpha \subseteq Cech_\alpha \subseteq Rips_{2\alpha}$, which is what makes the cheaper Rips complex a proxy for the geometrically faithful Čech complex (§2). The Nerve Theorem then converts covers into combinatorics: for a cover by open sets whose finite intersections are empty or contractible, the union and the nerve are homotopy equivalent (§2, Thm. 1), so the union of balls about a point cloud carries the same homology as its Čech complex.

Exploratory analysis (§3) applies that idea to a cover of the *image* of the data: with a filter function $f$ and a cover $\mathcal U$ of $f(X)$, one takes the refined pull-back cover (connected components of each $f^{-1}(U)$) and computes its nerve — Algorithm 1, the Mapper algorithm (§3, Def. 2). The output is usually a graph, and the paper deliberately follows it with a discussion of its user choices: the filter (density estimates, PCA or non-linear dimension-reduction coordinates, the centrality function $\sum_y d(x,y)$ or the eccentricity function $\max_y d(x,y)$, distance to a point for filamentary data); the cover (regularly spaced intervals of length $r$, the *resolution*, overlapping by a *gain* $g$; if $g<50\%$ each point lies in at most two cover elements and the nerve is a graph); and the clustering of preimages, by a per-set clusterer or as connected components of a $k$-NN or $\varepsilon$-graph (§3, pp. 8–9).

Geometric inference (§4) replaces arbitrary covers by a union of balls of radius $r$ about the sample $X_n$, a sample i.i.d. from a measure $\mu$ with compact support $M \subset \R^d$, and asks when the union (equivalently $Cech_r(X_n)$) has the topology of $M$. This needs regularity: the $r$-offset $K^r$ is the $r$-sublevel set of the distance function $d_K$, and the relevant functions are the *distance-like* ones, proper $\phi$ with $\|x\|^2 - \phi^2(x)$ convex (§4.1, Def. 4), whose gradient flow controls how sublevel-set topology changes (the Isotopy Lemma, Lemma 1). Regularity is quantified by the weak feature size $\mathrm{wfs}_\phi(r)$ and the $\alpha$-*reach* (§4.1, Def. 5), the latter generalising Federer's reach. The Reconstruction Theorem (Thm. 2) then says the sublevel sets of two close distance-like functions are homotopy equivalent on a range of radii, and its consequence for Betti numbers (Thm. 3) eliminates the choice of the geometric side: the Betti numbers of $Cech_r(X)$ equal those of the $\eta$-offset $M^\eta$ — and those of $M$ itself when $M$ is a smooth $m$-dimensional submanifold. Because building the Čech complex requires a delicate "is this union of balls empty?" predicate, a second result replaces it by Vietoris–Rips complexes and expresses $\beta_k$ as the rank of a map between two Rips complexes at scales $r$ and $4r$ (Thm. 4). Outlier robustness is obtained by replacing the distance to a set with the distance to a measure (DTM), a Wasserstein-stable average of quantile distances (§4.4).

The core (§5) is persistent homology. A filtration $(F_r)_{r \in T}$ is scanned as scale grows; features are given a birth and a death, collected into a persistence barcode, or equivalently a persistence diagram, a multiset of points above the diagonal (§5.2–5.3). The algebraic route defines a persistence module as the homology of the filtration with the maps induced by inclusion, and shows that finite or finite-dimensional modules decompose uniquely into interval modules, so the diagram is well defined (Thm. 5); for the non-decomposable cases one uses the weaker $q$-tame condition (Def. 8, Thm. 6). Two alternative encodings are emphasised: the persistence landscape, which maps a diagram into a Banach/Hilbert space of functions (§5.4), and more generally "linear representations" of a diagram as a sum of per-point vectors (§5.5), covering Betti curves and persistence surfaces/images. Diagrams are compared with the bottleneck distance $d_b$ (§5.6), whose matching formulation is the reason stability holds, and with Wasserstein analogues. Stability is proved once, algebraically, for $\delta$-interleaved modules (Thm. 7), from which the concrete results follow: sublevel sets of functions differing by $\varepsilon$ in sup norm give diagrams at distance $\le \varepsilon$ (Thm. 8), and filtrations of metric spaces give diagrams at distance $\le 2 d_{GH}$ (Thm. 9).

The statistical part (§6) reads these stability results as estimation statements: the map from data to diagram being Lipschitz, consistency of the diagram estimator follows from consistency of the support estimator under Hausdorff distance (§6.1), and confidence sets from confidence sets in the sample space (§6.2). The survey reviews the options — subsampling bands, the bottleneck bootstrap, bootstrap of persistent Betti numbers — then families of diagrams: Fréchet means, average landscapes from many subsamples (Thm. 11), asymptotic normality and the multiplier bootstrap (§6.3). Section 6.5 reviews the machine-learning uses: exploratory visualisation, feature engineering via landscapes, kernels and vector summaries, differentiable persistence layers, and persistence-based regularisers for model design.

## Key equations and definitions

The two complexes used throughout, with their interleaving (§2, p. 5):

$$
Rips_\alpha(X) \subseteq Cech_\alpha(X) \subseteq Rips_{2\alpha}(X)
$$

$Rips_\alpha(X)$ holds a simplex iff every pair of its vertices is at distance $\le \alpha$; $Cech_\alpha(X)$ holds it iff the closed $\alpha$-balls about its vertices meet. The inclusion lets one compute with Rips complexes while reasoning about Čech ones.

Simplicial homology with $\Z_2$ coefficients, and Betti numbers (§4.2, Def. 6, p. 13):

$$
H_k(K) = Z_k(K)/B_k(K), \qquad \beta_k(K) = \dim H_k(K)
$$

$Z_k$, $B_k$ are the $k$-cycles and $k$-boundaries; $H_0$ counts connected components, $H_1$ loops, $H_2$ cavities. The Nerve Theorem (Thm. 1, p. 6) states that if the finite intersections of an open cover are empty or contractible, the union and the nerve are homotopy equivalent.

The Reconstruction Theorem, whose hypotheses are the regularity condition that makes geometric inference possible (§4.1, Thm. 2, p. 11):

$$
\|\phi - \psi\|_\infty < \varepsilon,\quad \mathrm{reach}_\alpha(\phi) \ge R \;\Longrightarrow\; \psi_r \simeq \phi_\eta \ \text{ for } r \in [4\varepsilon/\alpha^2,\, R-3\varepsilon],\ \eta \in (0,R),\ \text{ provided } \varepsilon \le \frac{R}{5 + 4/\alpha^2}
$$

where $\phi_r = \phi^{-1}([0,r])$; taking $\phi = d_M$, $\psi = d_{X_n}$ relates the sample's offsets to the unknown support.

Betti numbers from Vietoris–Rips complexes, avoiding the Čech emptiness predicate (§4.2, Thm. 4, pp. 14–15):

$$
\mathrm{wfs}(M) \ge R > 0,\ d_H(M,X) = \varepsilon < \tfrac{1}{9}\mathrm{wfs}(M),\ r \in [2\varepsilon, \tfrac{1}{4}(\mathrm{wfs}(M)-\varepsilon)] \;\Longrightarrow\; \beta_k(X^\eta) = \mathrm{rk}\big(H_k(Rips_r(X)) \to H_k(Rips_{4r}(X))\big)
$$

with $X^\eta$ the $\eta$-offset of $X$; the multiscale strategy for choosing $r$ is deferred to Chazal and Oudot (2008, outside the collection).

The distance to measure and its stability (§4.4, Eq. 1–2, p. 16):

$$
d_{P,m,r}(x) := \left(\frac{1}{m}\int_0^m \delta^r_{P,u}(x)\,\mathrm{d}u\right)^{1/r}, \qquad \delta_{P,u}(x) = \inf\{t>0 : P(B(x,t)) \ge u\}
$$

$$
\|d_{P,m,r} - d_{\tilde P,m,r}\|_\infty \le m^{-1/r}\, W_r(P, \tilde P)
$$

i.e. $P \mapsto d_{P,m,r}$ is $m^{-1/r}$-Lipschitz for the Wasserstein metric; for $r=2$ the square $d^2_{P,m,2}$ is semiconcave. Its plug-in estimator on $n$ points, with $m = k/n$, is the DTEM $d^r_{P_n,k/n,r}(x) := \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^k \|x - X_n\|^r_{(j)}$ (§4.4, p. 17), computable from distances alone.

The bottleneck distance between diagrams (§5.6, pp. 25–26):

$$
d_b(\mathrm{dgm}_1, \mathrm{dgm}_2) = \inf_{\text{matching } m} \max_{(p,q) \in m} \|p-q\|_\infty
$$

a matching pairs every off-diagonal point of each diagram to a point of the other or to the diagonal $\Delta$; matching is a bipartite perfect-matching problem. Its Wasserstein analogue is $W_p(\mathrm{dgm}_1,\mathrm{dgm}_2)^p = \inf_m \sum_{(p,q)\in m}\|p-q\|_\infty^p$.

The persistence landscape tent function and $k$-th landscape (§5.4, Eq. 3–4, p. 23):

$$
\Lambda_p(t) = \begin{cases} t - b, & t \in [b, \tfrac{b+d}{2}] \\ d - t, & t \in (\tfrac{b+d}{2}, d] \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}
\qquad
\lambda_{\mathrm{dgm}}(k,t) = k\text{max}_{r \in \mathrm{dgm}}\, \Lambda_r(t), \quad t \in [0,T],\ k \in \N
$$

$k\text{max}$ is the $k$-th largest value; $\lambda(k,\cdot)$ is 1-Lipschitz, and the diagram-to-landscape map is injective. A linear representation of a diagram sums a vector-valued function of each point (§5.5, Eq. 5, p. 24): $\Psi_k(\mathrm{dgm}_k) = \sum_{p \in \mathrm{dgm}_k} f_k(p)$, with Betti curves, persistence silhouettes and persistence surfaces as special cases.

Stability in the Gromov–Hausdorff setting (§5.7, Thm. 9, p. 27):

$$
d_b\big(\mathrm{dgm}(\mathrm{Filt}(X)), \mathrm{dgm}(\mathrm{Filt}(Y))\big) \le 2\, d_{GH}(X, Y)
$$

for the Vietoris–Rips or Čech filtrations of two compact metric spaces; it descends from the algebraic statement that $\delta$-interleaved $q$-tame modules have diagrams at bottleneck distance at most $\delta$ (Thm. 7).

The (a,b)-standard assumption on the measure and the minimax rate it yields for diagram estimation (§6.1, Eq. 6, Thm. 10, p. 28):

$$
\mu(B(x,r)) \ge \min(a r^b, 1) \quad \forall x \in X_\mu,\ r>0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\sup_{\mu \in \mathcal P} \E\big[d_b\big(\mathrm{dgm}(\mathrm{Filt}(X_\mu)), \mathrm{dgm}(\mathrm{Filt}(X_n))\big)\big] \le C \left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{1/b}
$$

with $C$ depending only on $a,b$; the corresponding lower bound holds up to a logarithmic term.

## Main results

- **Cover-to-shape conversion (§2, Thm. 1).** The Nerve Theorem makes the union of balls about a point cloud homotopy equivalent to its Čech complex; combined with $Rips_\alpha \subseteq Cech_\alpha \subseteq Rips_{2\alpha}$ it justifies computing with Rips complexes. Mapper is the same mechanism applied to a filter function's pull-back cover, yielding a visualisable graph (§3, Algorithm 1).
- **Reconstruction and Betti-number estimation (§4.1–4.2, Thm. 2–4).** Under a reach condition on the support, the offsets $M^\eta$ and the Čech complex $Cech_r(X_n)$ of the sample have the same Betti numbers for a whole interval of radii, and for a smooth $m$-dimensional submanifold $\beta_k(Cech_r(X)) = \beta_k(M)$ for $k = 0,\dots,m$ (Thm. 3). Theorem 4 removes the Čech computation entirely, expressing $\beta_k(X^\eta)$ as the rank of the homomorphism $H_k(Rips_r(X)) \to H_k(Rips_{4r}(X))$.
- **Robustness to outliers (§4.4).** The DTM is $m^{-1/r}$-Lipschitz for the Wasserstein metric (Eq. 2), and $d^2_{P,m,2}$ is semiconcave, which is what makes DTM-based inference stable and DTM-filtrations possible.
- **Stability of persistence (§5.7, Thm. 7–9, Prop. 1).** $d_b \le \delta$ for $\delta$-interleaved modules; $d_b(\mathrm{dgm}_k(f),\mathrm{dgm}_k(g)) \le \|f-g\|_\infty$; $d_b \le 2 d_{GH}$ for Rips or Čech filtrations; and persistence landscapes are stable, $\lvert \lambda(k,t) - \lambda'(k,t) \rvert \le d_b(\mathrm{dgm},\mathrm{dgm}')$, and decreasing in $k$.
- **Statistics (§6).** Under the $(a,b)$-standard assumption, diagram estimation converges at the minimax rate $C(\log n / n)^{1/b}$ in bottleneck distance (Thm. 10), and confidence bands for diagrams can be read off confidence sets for the support; the bottleneck bootstrap gives an alternative, often less conservative, calibrated band (Eq. 7). For families of diagrams, average landscapes converge at the rates of Theorem 11, and persistent Betti numbers are asymptotically normal and bootstrappable (§6.2–6.3).
- **Two worked case studies (§7).** Protein binding: 14 configurations of the maltose-binding protein (370 amino acid residues) are compared through the *dynamical distance* $D_{ij} = 1 - \lvert C_{ij} \rvert$ built from residue correlation matrices $C$; a Rips complex (max edge length 1.1, dimension 2) gives diagrams whose bottleneck distances, embedded by multidimensional scaling, separate the closed and open forms (Fig. 13(c)), and a bootstrap 95% band is drawn on a diagram (Fig. 13(d)). Sensor classification: 3 walkers, 100 windows of 200 consecutive 3D acceleration samples (8 s) each, 300 series; alpha-complex persistence, the first three landscapes in dimensions 0 and 1 discretised on 1000 points each, give 6000 features per series, classified by a random forest with an "averaged classification error around 0.95" (§7.2, p. 36; see the critical assessment), with variable importances plotted (Fig. 14(b)).

## Limitations stated by the authors

- The paper is deliberately partial: "a brief introduction, through a few selected topics" (Abstract), and "providing a complete overview of all these existing approaches is beyond the scope of this introductory survey" (§1, p. 1). No proofs are given; the algebraic construction of persistence diagrams is left to Chazal et al. (2016a, outside the collection) (§5.3).
- Mapper is unstable: "the output of the Mapper is very sensitive to the choice of $\mathcal U$ and small changes in the resolution and gain parameters may results in very large changes in the output, making the method very unstable" (§3, p. 9).
- Geometric inference carries three practical difficulties (§4.2, p. 14): the $\alpha$-reach regularity "may be too restrictive"; computing the nerve of a union of balls "requires the use of a tricky predicate testing the emptiness of a finite union of balls"; and "the estimation of the Betti numbers relies on the scale parameter $r$ whose choice may be a problem". Theorem 4 does not settle the last one, only helping via a multiscale strategy described elsewhere.
- Confidence regions derived from stability "may lead to very conservative confidence regions" (§6.2, p. 30); the bottleneck bootstrap "should be considered with caution because as far as we know the validity of the bottleneck bootstrap has not been proved in this framework" (§7.1, p. 35).
- Persistence diagrams are "highly non linear", so they cannot be used directly as features; the relevant topological information is often localised, which "usually makes the choice of a relevant kernel or vector summary very difficult for the user" (§6.5, p. 33).
- Stability and discriminative power trade off: continuity is only available for weight functions that vanish near the diagonal, while "in general there is no specific reason to consider that points close to the diagonal are less important than others" (§5.7, p. 28).

## Critical assessment

The value of this paper is tutorial, not technical: it introduces no new theorem, and every result in it is attributed to its source. Judged on that purpose it succeeds — the pipeline structure, the grouping of stability results under one algebraic statement (Thm. 7), and the explicit decision to replace Čech by Rips complexes (Thm. 4) are exactly the things a newcomer needs, and §7 makes the abstract machinery runnable with public Gudhi code and two public datasets.

Two weaknesses stand out. First, the empirical evidence is thin: the protein example is reported only through an MDS picture (Fig. 13(c)) with no distance values, and the sensor experiment gives a single random forest, no baseline (a walker-classification task with three classes is a strong candidate for trivial classical features), and one number. That number is reported as "an averaged classification error around 0.95" (§7.2, p. 36); taken literally this is worse than chance for three classes, so either "error" means accuracy (reading it as an accuracy of ≈0.95 would make the paragraph coherent) or something is off — the extracted text gives 0.95 and I found no way to check the rendered page (this model has no vision, and there is no scanned text layer to fall back on). The same section shows a second slip: the empirical average landscape is introduced as an average over $\ell$ subsamples "$\lambda^m_\ell(t) = \frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\lambda_{S^m_i}(t)$" (Eq. 8, p. 31), with $b$ undefined — evidently $\ell$ was meant (sic).

Second, the selection of topics is idiosyncratic in a way the paper acknowledges but does not fully repair: a reader who wants to *use* persistent homology gets the definitions and the stability theory, but nothing is said about the algorithm that computes persistence pairs, and the choice of the scale parameter — flagged as a difficulty in §4.2 — remains effectively unanswered. The statistics of §6 is also a tour of results rather than a recipe: three confidence-band constructions are listed with no guidance on which to trust, and the two invoked in §7 are precisely the ones whose validity the authors call unproven or conservative.

None of this undermines the central message, which has aged well: the stability theorems ($d_b \le \|f-g\|_\infty$, $d_b \le 2 d_{GH}$) are why topological summaries can be treated as statistical estimators at all, and what makes persistence usable as a feature map rather than a visualisation toy.

## Connections within the collection

This survey is the collection's reference for the TDA toolchain several papers use. The most direct consumer is [@liuReLUNeuralNetworks2023], which feeds the dual graph of a ReLU network's polyhedral decomposition to persistent homology: that is exactly the pipeline of §1, with the network's cell decomposition supplying the complex instead of a point cloud. The same piecewise-linear-manifold picture of network preimages is computed explicitly in [@carlssonPREIMAGERECTIFIERNETWORK2017] — a different route to the geometric structure this survey abstracts, so the relation is one of shared viewpoint rather than citation.

Other papers sit closer to individual sections. [@masdenAlgorithmicDeterminationCombinatorial2022] and [@berzinsPolyhedralComplexExtraction2023] build and enumerate the polyhedral complex a ReLU network induces on input space; a polyhedral complex is the more general relative of the simplicial complexes of §2, and the counting literature ([@NIPS2014_fa6f2a46], [@pascanuNumberResponseRegions2013], [@serraBoundingCountingLinear2018], [@goujonNumberRegionsPiecewise2023]) asks combinatorial-complexity questions about that same object. [@meilaManifoldLearningWhat2023] is the closest companion survey, covering the non-linear dimension-reduction tradition that this paper's geometric inference (§4) and Mapper filters (§3) presuppose. For latent-space work in the collection — [@arvanitidisGeometricallyEnrichedLatent2020], [@pouplinIdentifyingLatentDistances2023], [@fuLatentTopologyInduction2022] — the relevant export is the stability-and-estimation mindset: topological descriptors survive Gromov–Hausdorff or Wasserstein perturbations, so they can serve as learned-feature summaries, though none of these papers invokes persistent homology directly. [@nielsenElementaryIntroductionInformation2020] plays the analogous role for information geometry rather than topology.
