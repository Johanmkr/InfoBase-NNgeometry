# Towards Fully Covariant Machine Learning

**Villar, Hogg, Yao, Kevrekidis, Schölkopf (2023)**, *arXiv preprint (under review at TMLR)*. [arXiv:2301.13724](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13724)

!!! abstract "TL;DR"
    Every dataset is written down in some choice of coordinates, units and labelling, and the physics or
    semantics of the problem does not depend on that choice. The paper names the symmetries induced by these
    investigator choices *passive symmetries* and argues they are exact, universal and largely ignored by
    machine learning, in contrast to the empirical *active symmetries* the equivariant-ML literature studies.
    It gives a formal definition in terms of commuting diagrams, shows with two toy regressions that enforcing
    passive covariance can recover a hidden physical constant (Planck's constant) and a hidden vector (gravity),
    and lists concrete practice violations — normalization, $L_1$/$L_\infty$ objectives, kernel choices.

## Problem and motivation

Two kinds of symmetry are at work in a learning problem (§1). *Active* symmetries are empirical regularities of
the physical law — the claim that protein folding does not depend on the molecule's orientation is an
observation, approximate and falsifiable. *Passive* symmetries come from the arbitrariness of the mathematical
representation: a coordinate system was chosen, a unit system was chosen, graph nodes were ordered. These are
exact and true by definition, because nothing physical changes when the description is changed (§1, §3). The
authors' claim is that passive symmetries are in play in *essentially all* data-analysis problems, yet almost all
of equivariant ML targets active symmetries — plausibly because in most problems the coordinate system is fixed
before the problem is posed and both train and test data share it (§3).

The paper is explicitly conceptual (§1, §11): it contributes a translation between the languages of physics,
mathematics and machine learning (a ~20-entry glossary, §2), a formal definition, two demonstrations, and a set
of *dos and don'ts* (§1). The motivation is historical: enforcing passive symmetries is what turned physics into
a predictive theory — coordinate freedom, gauge freedom and units covariance each produced fundamental results,
and general relativity was found by scanning generally covariant equations until one matched the weak-field
limit (§3).

A central practical difficulty is that a passive symmetry only becomes enforceable when *all* relevant objects
are in the data. If the gravity vector, or Planck's constant, is missing from the feature list, the exact
symmetry in play is only the subgroup $H$ fixing the missing object, and an $H$-equivariant model cannot
generalize to regimes where that object changes (§11). Finding the missing elements is presented as structurally
the same problem as finding confounders in causal inference (§3, §7).

## Method

1. **Define the symmetry.** Fix a state space $X$, a space $H$ of encodings (lists of real components in some
   coordinate and unit system), and maps $\Phi_i: X \to H$. If every $\Phi_i$ records all the information
   describing $x$, then for any pair of encodings there is an invertible morphism $\beta_{ji}: H \to H$ making
   Diagram (2) commute; the group $P$ generated by all such $\beta_{ji}$ is the passive symmetry group (§5).
   Active symmetries are instead defined by fixing one encoding $\Phi$, a function $F: H \to Y$ and a group $G$
   acting on $X, H, Y$ by $\tau, \beta, \rho$; the tuple $(G, F, \beta, \rho)$ is an active symmetry when
   Diagram (3) commutes (§5).
2. **Enforce it by construction.** Once the symmetry and all contextual inputs are known, the model should be
   exactly equivariant to $G$: change the representation of the inputs, the representation of the output must
   change correspondingly (§3). The paper endorses the two standard routes to exact equivariance — explicit
   equivariant layers/weight sharing, or expressing the model in a generating set of invariant features (§8) —
   and the Ricci-calculus discipline of writing every function from scalars, vectors and tensors by index
   contraction, which makes covariance true by construction (§3).
3. **Demonstrate on toy problems** where a hidden constant must be recovered, by including a learnable object
   with the right units or the right transformation law as an extra input feature and requiring exact covariance
   with respect to it (§6).
4. **Audit the standard toolbox** against the symmetry: data normalization, batch/layer normalization, kernel
   functions, $L_1$/$L_\infty$ norms, flat-minimum regularizers, and the units/graph constraints that neural
   network weights silently carry (§9, §10).

## Key equations and definitions

Equivariance, the property the whole paper is about (§2, Eq. 1):

$$
f\bigl(\rho_X(g)(x)\bigr) = \rho_Y(g)\bigl(f(x)\bigr)
\qquad \text{(Eq. 1, §2)}
$$

with $G$ acting on $X$ and $Y$ through representations $\rho_X, \rho_Y$; invariance is the special case where
the output action is trivial (§2). The actions induce an action on maps,

$$
\rho_{XY}(g)(f) = \rho_Y(g) \circ f \circ \rho_X(g)^{-1},
\qquad \text{(§2, p. 4)}
$$

so that equivariant maps are the fixed points of this action.

Passive symmetry: the group $P$ of invertible morphisms $\beta_{ji}$ for which the encodings commute,
$\Phi_j = \beta_{ji} \circ \Phi_i$ (Diagram 2, §5), i.e. the possible changes of units, coordinates, and
automorphisms between encodings. Active symmetry: $(G, F, \beta, \rho)$ such that $F \circ \beta(g) \circ \Phi
= \rho(g) \circ F \circ \Phi$ for all $g \in G$ (Diagram 3, §5). Mathematically the two look alike; they differ
in epistemological content — passive transformations are changes of the description, active ones are
interventions on the system (§3, §5).

Units covariance is illustrated by pure dimensional analysis (§4). Dropping a mass $m$ from height $h$ under
constant gravity $g$, the only combination with units of time is

$$
T = \alpha\sqrt{h/g}, \qquad\text{and for a projectile launched at speed } v \text{ at angle } \theta:
\qquad L = \beta(\theta)\,v^2/g,
\qquad \text{(§4)}
$$

with dimensionless $\alpha$ and dimensionless function $\beta(\theta)$. Both answers are forced by units alone,
with no training data, and neither depends on $m$ — the observation that leads to general relativity (§4).

Planck's law, the target of the first experiment (Eq. 4, §6):

$$
B_\lambda(\lambda) = \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{hc}{\lambda k T}\right) - 1\right]^{-1}
\qquad \text{(Eq. 4, §6)}
$$

The pre-quantum dimensionally acceptable candidate $B_\lambda(\lambda) = 2ckT/\lambda^4$ is the long-wavelength
limit; the ultraviolet catastrophe is resolved by units covariance only once a new constant with units of action
is admitted (§6).

Naive feature normalization, the object of §10 (Eq. 5):

$$
X' \leftarrow \sigma^{-1}(X - \mu),
\qquad \text{(Eq. 5, §10)}
$$

where $\sigma$ and $\mu$ are scales and shifts derived from the training features. Applied component-wise this
violates the passive symmetries: components of a vector may not be averaged or scaled independently, features of
different units may not be combined, and tensor components may not be treated on equal footing (§10).

## Main results

**Black-body radiation (§6, Fig. 2 left).** Noisy samples of intensity as a function of wavelength and
temperature are generated from Eq. 4; the task is to predict intensity. Three models, all 3-layer MLPs with 20
hidden units: a units-covariant regression using only $(\lambda, T, c, k)$ reaches test RMSE $6.35$; the same
units-covariant regression with an extra learnable dimensional constant reaches RMSE $0.39$; a standard MLP with
no units constraint reaches RMSE $0.64$ (§6, Fig. 2 caption). The constant selected by low validation error is
$1.61\mathrm{e}52\ \mathrm{kg}^{-1}\,\mathrm{m}^{-1}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\,\mathrm{K}^{-1}$ — the same units as, and
a similar magnitude to, $ckh^{-2}$ (§6, Fig. 2 caption). The authors read this as units covariance plus a
learnable constant recovering Planck's constant from data.

**Springy double pendulum (§6, Fig. 2 right; App. A).** The dissipationless double pendulum with springs has
kinetic and potential energy (Eq. 6, Eq. 7, App. A); positions and momenta of both masses are predicted $T = 5$
steps ahead from $N = 500$ initializations, with test evaluation at $T = 150$ time steps (App. A, Eq. 8). Three
$O(3)$-equivariant models, built in the space of invariant scalars by Ricci calculus: *Known-g* (gravity as an
input feature), *No-g* (gravity absent), and *Learned-g* (an extra unknown vector feature learned jointly with
the weights). The learned vector came out nearly parallel to the true gravity vector, with an angle of
$0.00016$ radians between them (§6). The authors conclude that $O(3)$ equivariance permits the gravity vector to
be learned from data with only minimal impact on performance (§6, Fig. 2 caption).

**Audit of practice (§9, §10).** Kernels built as exponentials of sums of squares over features with different
units cannot be units covariant; an $O(3)$-covariant scalar objective must combine vector components only
through Euclidean norms; $L_1$ and $L_\infty$ norms are generally non-covariant; flat-minimum regularizers
change value under weight transformations that leave the function invariant; data, batch and layer normalization
are "generally brutal" and violate the equivariances and covariances in play (§9). Nonlinearities such as
sigmoid or tanh can only act on dimensionless scalars, which forces implicit unit and geometric structure onto
the weights of the first and last layers and constrains the network's graph structure — with the notable
exception of homogeneous nonlinearities, of which ReLU is one (§9). The authors raise as an open question
whether the success of ReLU is related to its homogeneity (§9).

## Limitations stated by the authors

Enforcement is often impractical: full coordinate diffeomorphisms and problem reparameterizations involve groups
too large to implement, and exactness requires that *all* relevant quantities, including unknown fundamental
constants, are in the problem (§11). Identifiability problems may arise when several constants or
non-constant features are missing (§11). The demonstrations are toy problems with rich training data and the
symmetry group known in advance (§6, §11); the paper is conceptual and offers "no statistical" machinery, and
its conjecture that enforcing passive symmetries improves ML is explicitly stated as a conjecture (§5, §3).
Some passive symmetries are hard even to recognize: it can be difficult to tell whether a symmetry is active or
passive (§3). A third class, *observer symmetries* (the freedom in how a data record was taken), is approximate,
admits no group in the space of data, and is left as future work (§11).

## Critical assessment

The paper's real content is a vocabulary plus a set of warnings, and it is worth taking on those terms. The
separation of passive from active symmetry is genuinely useful framing and cleanly stated in §5; a reader should
notice, though, that the formal definitions only become actionable once one fixes which transformations count as
"representation changes", and the paper concedes that this determination is itself an empirical, causal-looking
inference (§3, §7). The evidential base is thin relative to the size of the claim: two toy experiments, both
with synthetic data generated from the very equation the model must recover, and neither with error bars,
multiple seeds or baselines beyond a single unconstrained MLP. The black-body comparison in particular is a
three-way RMSE comparison read from Fig. 2 with no confidence intervals, so "units covariance with an extra
constant outperforms the baseline MLP" (§6) is a $0.64 \to 0.39$ gap of unknown significance. The pendulum
result is more persuasive because the recovered vector is checked geometrically ($0.00016$ rad), but the
"minimal impact on performance" claim rests on a figure I could not inspect — the extracted text preserves the
panel labels but not the curves, so the shape of the comparison is unverified here. The neural-network, kernel
and normalization critiques in §9–§10 are argued rather than tested; the claim that a trained network "cannot be
covariant in the end, unless the inputs and outputs are already covariant scalars" (§9) is a sharp statement
presented without proof, and the homogeneity-of-ReLU speculation is offered as an open question rather than a
finding. Code for both experiments is released (§ Acknowledgements), which lowers the reproduction barrier
considerably. Overall this reads as a well-aimed agenda paper: the value is in the checklist, not in the
benchmarks, and the natural next step — quantifying the generalization gain from passive covariance — is left
entirely open.

## Connections within the collection

The paper positions itself as extending the equivariance program of geometric deep learning, which it cites as
its main ML precedent for exact symmetry by construction [@bronsteinGeometricDeepLearning] and contrasts with
the active-symmetry focus of that literature (§1, §3, §8). The distinction it draws — exact symmetry by
definition versus empirical invariance — is the conceptual axis it uses to separate its own passive symmetries
from the group-theoretic equivariance used across the rest of the collection.

Its treatment of latent-variable models and identifiability, where unobserved latent factors carry a large class
of gauge transformations (permutations, rotations, coordinate-wise nonlinear transformations) that must be
handled correctly (§9), is possibly related to the information-geometric account of statistical models and their
reparameterization-invariant structure in [@nielsenElementaryIntroductionInformation2020] and to the discussion
of manifolds and latent-coordinate freedom in [@meilaManifoldLearningWhat2023] — both introduce the same kind of
representation freedom from a different starting point. The equivariance and symmetry formalism itself
(group actions, representations, invariance as trivial output action) is standard background shared with the
geometric papers of the collection rather than something this paper builds on.
